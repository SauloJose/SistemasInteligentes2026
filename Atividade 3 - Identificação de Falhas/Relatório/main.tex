\begin{abstract}
A detecção precoce de falhas em processos industriais é crucial para manter a confiabilidade do sistema, segurança e eficiência operacional. Este trabalho apresenta um estudo abrangente de algoritmos de detecção de falhas baseados em oito metodologias distintas: métodos estatísticos multivariados (PCA, DPCA), abordagens supervisionadas (PLS, TPLS, MPLS, FDA) e técnicas baseadas em independência estatística (ICA, MICA). O trabalho avalia a eficácia de cada método na detecção de falhas incipientes e abruptas utilizando o benchmark do processo Tennessee Eastman. Os resultados demonstram trade-offs significativos entre a taxa de detecção (sensibilidade) e a taxa de alarmes falsos (especificidade), com DPCA apresentando o melhor equilíbrio geral (72,6\% FDR, 6,56\% FAR), enquanto MICA alcança a menor taxa de alarmes falsos (0,52\%). A análise comparativa fornece uma base para seleção de métodos adequados conforme os requisitos específicos da aplicação industrial.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introdução}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{rocessos} industriais operam em condições complexas e frequentemente imprevisíveis, onde falhas de equipamentos podem levar a perdas econômicas significativas, riscos à segurança e danos ambientais. A detecção oportuna de falhas incipientes permite manutenção preventiva, reduz tempo de parada não planejado e melhora a confiabilidade geral do sistema. Abordagens tradicionais de detecção de falhas baseadas em modelos requerem modelos matemáticos precisos do sistema, que frequentemente não estão disponíveis ou são difíceis de obter na prática. Consequentemente, métodos baseados em dados emergiram como alternativas viáveis que exploram dados históricos de processo para aprender padrões normais de operação e identificar desvios.

Este trabalho enfoca o desenvolvimento e comparação de oito abordagens distintas de detecção de falhas baseadas em dados em processos industriais. A Seção~\ref{sec:theoretical} fornece revisão abrangente dos fundamentos teóricos e trabalhos relacionados. A Seção~\ref{sec:methodology} descreve a formulação do problema e detalhes de implementação. A Seção~\ref{sec:results} apresenta resultados experimentais e análise comparativa. \subsection{Processos Industriais e Monitoramento Multivariável}
\label{subsec:industrial_processes}

Processos industriais modernos geram quantidades vastas de dados de sensores de múltiplas variáveis operando simultaneamente. Um processo multivariável típico pode ser representado como uma coleção de $m$ variáveis medidas amostradas em instantes discretos de tempo $k = 1, 2, \ldots, N$:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times N} = [\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{y}_N]
\label{eq:process_data}
\end{equation}

onde cada coluna $\mathbf{y}_k = [y_{1,k}, y_{2,k}, \ldots, y_{m,k}]^T$ representa um snapshot do estado do processo no instante $k$. O monitoramento de processo geralmente se concentra em detectar quando o sistema se desvia das condições normais de operação, o que pode indicar o início de uma falha.

\subsection{Fault Detection Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:fault_detection}

The general fault detection problem can be formulated as a binary classification task. Given process data $\mathbf{Y}$, the objective is to construct a decision rule that classifies observations as either normal (nominal) or abnormal (faulty):

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_k) = \begin{cases} 
\text{Normal} & \text{if} \quad \mathcal{T}_k \leq \tau \\
\text{Faulty} & \text{if} \quad \mathcal{T}_k > \tau
\end{cases}
\label{eq:decision_rule}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{T}_k$ is a test statistic computed from the observation $\mathbf{y}_k$, and $\tau$ is a threshold determined based on the desired false positive rate. The challenge lies in selecting appropriate test statistics and thresholds that maximize fault detection rate while minimizing false alarms.

\subsection{Statistical Detection Metrics: T² and SPE}
\label{subsec:statistical_metrics}

Most process monitoring methods rely on two fundamental statistical metrics computed from multivariate data: the Hotelling $T^2$ statistic and the Squared Prediction Error (SPE), also known as the Q-statistic.

\subsubsection{Hotelling T² Statistic}

The Hotelling $T^2$ statistic measures the distance of a point from the center of the training data in terms of the principal component subspace. For a new observation $\mathbf{x}_k$, after projection onto the first $a$ principal components:

\begin{equation}
T^2_k = \mathbf{t}_k^\top \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} \mathbf{t}_k
\label{eq:t2_statistic}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{t}_k$ are the scores (projections) and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_a)$ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues corresponding to the retained components. The $T^2$ statistic follows an $F$-distribution under the null hypothesis of normal operation:

\begin{equation}
T^2_{\text{lim}} = \frac{a(n-1)}{n-a} F_{\alpha}(a, n-a)
\label{eq:t2_limit}
\end{equation}

where $n$ is the number of training samples, $a$ is the number of retained components, and $F_{\alpha}$ is the critical value at significance level $\alpha$.

\subsubsection{Squared Prediction Error (Q-Statistic)}

The SPE (or Q-statistic) measures the contribution of each observation to the residual subspace orthogonal to the model subspace:

\begin{equation}
Q_k = \|\mathbf{x}_k - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k\|^2 = \|\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{x}_k \mathbf{P}_a \mathbf{P}_a^\top\|^2
\label{eq:spe_statistic}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{P}_a$ contains the first $a$ principal directions. The SPE is sensitive to faults that lie outside the model subspace. Its control limit is typically determined using the Jackson-Mudholkar approximation:

\begin{equation}
Q_{\text{lim}} = \theta_1 \left[ \frac{c_\alpha \sqrt{2\theta_2 h_0^2}}{\theta_1} + 1 + \frac{\theta_2 h_0 (h_0-1)}{\theta_1^2} \right]^{1/h_0}
\label{eq:spe_limit}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_k = \sum_{i=a+1}^p \lambda_i^k$ and $h_0 = 1 - \frac{2\theta_1 \theta_3}{3\theta_2^2}$.

\subsection{Comparative Overview of Fault Detection Methods}
\label{subsec:methods_overview}

This work compares eight different fault detection methodologies applied to the TE process. Each method employs different transformation strategies and statistical foundations:

\subsubsection{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}

PCA is the baseline multivariate statistical method that decomposes process data into uncorrelated principal components ordered by variance contribution. The method:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Computes the covariance matrix from training data
    \item Extracts eigenvectors (loadings) and eigenvalues
    \item Monitors both $T^2$ and SPE statistics on test data
    \item Declares faults when either statistic exceeds its control limit
\end{enumerate}

Advantages: computational efficiency, interpretability, and explicit reconstruction. Disadvantages: assumes linear relationships and requires careful selection of component number.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Principal Component Analysis (DPCA)}

DPCA extends PCA by incorporating temporal dynamics through lagged variables. Instead of using only the current observation $\mathbf{x}_k$, DPCA constructs an augmented vector:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}^{\text{aug}}_k = [\mathbf{x}_k^\top, \mathbf{x}_{k-1}^\top, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{k-\ell}^\top]^\top
\label{eq:dpca_augmented}
\end{equation}

where $\ell$ is the number of lagged samples. This captures process dynamics and improves detection of slowly evolving faults. The PCA model is then applied to this augmented space.

\subsubsection{Partial Least Squares (PLS)}

PLS combines the dimension reduction capability of PCA with supervised learning by relating process variables (X) to a quality output (y):

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{P}^\top + \mathbf{E}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{T} \mathbf{q} + \mathbf{f}
\label{eq:pls_model}
\end{equation}

PLS extracts latent variables that are simultaneously predictive of the output and representative of the input variation. For the TE process, the product quality (XMEAS 35, component G) serves as the quality output. Monitoring proceeds using $T^2$ and SPE statistics analogously to PCA.

\subsubsection{Time-Aware Partial Least Squares (TPLS)}

TPLS extends PLS by augmenting the process variables with temporal lags, similar to DPCA:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}^{\text{aug}} = [\mathbf{X}_k, \mathbf{X}_{k-1}, \ldots, \mathbf{X}_{k-\ell}]
\label{eq:tpls_augmented}
\end{equation}

This hybrid approach combines the supervised nature of PLS with dynamic information capture.

\subsubsection{Multivariate Partial Least Squares (MPLS)}

MPLS further extends PLS by considering multiple outputs simultaneously or through iterative refinement of the latent variable structure, enhancing the predictive power for complex quality relationships.

\subsubsection{Independent Component Analysis (ICA)}

ICA seeks to decompose the process data into statistically independent components rather than uncorrelated ones:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{S}
\label{eq:ica_model}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{S}$ contains independent components and $\mathbf{A}$ is the mixing matrix. ICA is based on the assumption that faults manifest as independent "source signals." The method can detect faults that lie in directions missed by PCA.

\subsubsection{Multivariate Independent Component Analysis (MICA)}

MICA combines ICA with temporal augmentation, similar to the relationship between DPCA and PCA. This captures both independence and temporal dynamics for improved detection sensitivity.

\subsubsection{Fisher Discriminant Analysis (FDA)}

FDA is a supervised dimensionality reduction method that seeks directions maximizing the ratio of between-class variance to within-class variance:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{w}} \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_W \mathbf{w}}
\label{eq:fda_criterion}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{S}_B$ is the between-class scatter matrix and $\mathbf{S}_W$ is the within-class scatter matrix. For FDA applied to the TE process, the two classes are "normal" and "faulty," enabling targeted discrimination.

\section{Methodology and Implementation}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section describes the implementation framework for both fault detection approaches, detailing data preprocessing, model development, and evaluation strategies.

\subsection{Problem Description and Dataset Characteristics}
\label{subsec:problem_description}

The fault detection algorithms are evaluated using the Tennessee Eastman (TE) process benchmark. Training data contains only nominal (fault-free) process samples ($n_{\text{train}} = 500$ samples), while test data includes both normal and faulty observations ($n_{\text{test}} = 960$ samples per fault scenario) corresponding to 21 different fault modes (IDV 1--21). All variables are normalized to zero mean and unit variance using statistics computed from the training set.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Feature Selection}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

Raw sensor data are normalized as:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\text{normalized}} = \frac{\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ are computed from training data. For methods incorporating temporal information (DPCA, TPLS, MICA), lagged variables are concatenated:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}^{\text{aug}}_k = [\mathbf{X}_k^\top, \mathbf{X}_{k-1}^\top, \ldots, \mathbf{X}_{k-\ell}^\top]^\top
\label{eq:augmented_features}
\end{equation}

with lag parameter $\ell = 2$ samples.

\subsection{Implementation of Individual Methods}
\label{subsec:implementation_summary}

\subsubsection{PCA and DPCA}

PCA is implemented via singular value decomposition (SVD) of the centered training data matrix. The number of components is selected to retain 95\% of total variance. For each test observation, $T^2$ and Q-statistics are computed and compared against control limits derived from the $F$-distribution and Jackson-Mudholkar approximation, respectively. DPCA follows the identical procedure applied to the temporally augmented feature space.

\subsubsection{PLS, TPLS, and MPLS}

PLS decomposes the relationship between process variables (X) and product quality (XMEAS 35, component G concentration) into latent variables. The model is trained to maximize covariance between X and y using the NIPALS algorithm. Components are retained based on cross-validation. TPLS applies PLS to temporally augmented X data. MPLS employs an improved latent variable extraction strategy through multiple objectives. Detection proceeds via the same $T^2$ and Q-statistics as PCA.

\subsubsection{ICA and MICA}

ICA is implemented using the FastICA algorithm to extract statistically independent components from the training data. Assuming linear mixing ($\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{S}$), the mixing matrix $\mathbf{A}$ and source components $\mathbf{S}$ are estimated such that components are mutually independent. For test observations, independence-based statistics are computed. MICA extends this by augmenting the data with temporal lags before ICA decomposition.

\subsubsection{Fisher Discriminant Analysis (FDA)}

FDA is trained on labeled data (normal vs. faulty samples) to find projection directions maximizing class separation. The discriminant subspace projects all observations; faults are identified when the projection lies far from the normal class center.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Model performance is evaluated using standard classification metrics:

\begin{align}
\text{Sensitivity} &= \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} \label{eq:sensitivity} \\
\text{Specificity} &= \frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN} + \text{FP}} \label{eq:specificity} \\
\text{F1-score} &= 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Sensitivity}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Sensitivity}} \label{eq:f1}
\end{align}

where TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.

\textbf{Strong Detection Faults (FDR $>$ 95\%):} IDV(01), IDV(02), IDV(04), IDV(06), IDV(07), IDV(12), IDV(14). These represent abrupt faults that significantly perturb process measurements or composition. Most methods detect these reliably.

\subsubsection{PCA and DPCA}

Both methods achieve excellent FAR (5.6\% and 6.6\%) and strong performance on abrupt faults ($>$95\% FDR). However, both methods struggle equally with difficult faults (IDV(03), IDV(09), IDV(15)).

\subsubsection{PLS Methods}

PLS, TPLS, and MPLS show higher FAR (10--12\%) but improved FDR on difficult faults through supervised learning. TPLS and MPLS achieve notable improvements on IDV(05) (37.4\% and 36.5\%) and IDV(10) (77.2\% and 90.0\%). MPLS delivers the highest FDR for IDV(10) (90.0\%), demonstrating the value of multivariate output considerations.

\subsubsection{ICA and MICA}

ICA maintains moderate FAR (7.0\%) and performs well on abrupt faults ($>$90\% FDR). MICA achieves exceptionally low FAR (0.52\%), the best among all methods, but at the cost of reduced FDR for difficult faults (e.g., IDV(03): 3.4\%, IDV(09): 1.6\%). This reflects a conservative detection strategy.

\subsubsection{FDA}

FDA exhibits the highest variance in performance: excellent for some faults (IDV(04), IDV(05), IDV(06): 100\%, 99.9\%, 100\%) and very poor for others (IDV(03), IDV(09), IDV(15), IDV(19), IDV(21): $<$2\%). This suggests FDA is highly sensitive to class imbalance and training data composition.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:summary}

This work presents a comprehensive benchmark comparison of eight distinct data-driven fault detection methodologies applied to the Tennessee Eastman process. The study evaluated both classical multivariate statistical methods (PCA, DPCA) and advanced techniques incorporating temporal dynamics (ICA, MICA) and supervised learning objectives (PLS variants, FDA). The comparative analysis reveals that no single method dominates across all performance metrics, highlighting the trade-off between sensitivity (fault detection rate) and specificity (false alarm rate).

\subsection{Key Findings}
\label{subsec:key_findings}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Trade-off Between FDR and FAR}: MICA achieves the lowest false alarm rate (0.52\%) but at reduced detection sensitivity for difficult faults (average FDR 62.3\%). Conversely, DPCA achieves the highest average FDR (72.6\%) while maintaining reasonable FAR (6.56\%).
    
    \item \textbf{Temporal Information Improves Performance}: Temporal extensions (DPCA vs. PCA, TPLS vs. PLS, MICA vs. ICA) generally improve FDR on slow-evolving or subtle faults, justifying the added computational cost for many applications.
    
    \item \textbf{Fault Difficulty Varies}: Abrupt, magnitude-significant faults (IDV(01), IDV(02), IDV(04), IDV(06), IDV(07)) are detected reliably (FDR $>$95\%) by all methods. Subtle faults with minimal measurement impact (IDV(03), IDV(09), IDV(15)) remain challenging, with FDR often below 25\%.
    
    \item \textbf{Supervised Methods Show Variable Performance}: While PLS-based methods achieve competitive average FDR (70.1--71.8\%), FDA exhibits high variance, excelling on some faults while failing completely on others. This reflects sensitivity to training data composition and class imbalance.
    
    \item \textbf{Method Selection Depends on Application Requirements}: For critical infrastructure requiring high specificity, MICA is optimal. For general-purpose monitoring emphasizing detection coverage, DPCA is recommended. For quality-focused applications, TPLS or MPLS are appropriate.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Recommendations for Practice}
\label{subsec:recommendations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-Method Deployment}: Choose DPCA as a reliable baseline offering 72.6\% average FDR with 6.56\% FAR.
    \item \textbf{Ensemble Approach}: Combine multiple methods (e.g., DPCA + MICA) to balance sensitivity and specificity. Flag alarms when consensus is achieved.
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Threshold Tuning}: Allow threshold adjustment based on process operation mode and maintenance policy priorities.
    \item \textbf{Fault-Specific Configuration}: For known-critical faults, calibrate methods individually rather than applying uniform thresholds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Research Directions}
\label{subsec:future_work}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hybrid and Ensemble Methods}: Develop intelligent fusion strategies that adaptively weight multiple methods based on process state and historical performance.
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning Approaches}: Investigate recurrent neural networks (LSTM, GRU) and attention mechanisms to capture temporal dependencies more effectively than traditional lagged-variable approaches.
    \item \textbf{Kernel Methods}: Explore kernel extensions (KPCA, KPLS) to capture nonlinear process manifolds.
    \item \textbf{Transfer Learning}: Investigate domain adaptation to enable knowledge transfer from well-studied processes (like TE) to different industrial applications.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Implementation}: Assess computational requirements for embedded deployment and develop online updating mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Root Cause Analysis}: Extend detection systems with isolation and identification modules to guide maintenance decisions.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty Quantification}: Incorporate Bayesian frameworks to quantify confidence in fault detection decisions and reduce false alarms.
\end{itemize}

The ultimate objective is to develop adaptive, multi-method fault detection systems capable of operating reliably across diverse industrial environments with minimal manual configuration while maintaining high reliability standards.
